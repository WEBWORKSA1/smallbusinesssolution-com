Small business funding options, compared

The cheapest money is the money matched to the right purpose. Here's what each option is for, what it costs, and who qualifies.

Match the funding to the purpose

  • Long-term assets (equipment, vehicles, property) → long-term loans or equipment finance.
  • Short-term gaps (seasonality, slow-paying clients) → lines of credit or invoice financing.
  • Growth with uncertain payback (new products, expansion) → owner equity, investors, or grants.

Using short-term, expensive money for long-term needs is one of the fastest ways to create a cash crisis.

1. Term loans

A lump sum repaid in fixed monthly instalments, from banks, credit unions and online lenders. Banks usually offer the lowest rates but want time in business, solid credit and financial statements. Online lenders decide faster but often charge more. Model payments with the loan calculator.

2. Government-backed loans

Many countries partially guarantee small business loans to encourage lending — for example the SBA loan programs in the US, the Canada Small Business Financing Program, and the British Business Bank's schemes in the UK. Rates and terms are often favourable; paperwork and approval time are heavier.

3. Business lines of credit

A revolving limit you draw on and repay as needed, paying interest only on what you use. Ideal for cash-flow gaps. Open one while your numbers are healthy; it's much harder to get when you urgently need it.

4. Equipment financing and leasing

The equipment itself secures the loan, which makes approval easier. Compare the total cost of financing with leasing, especially for technology that will be outdated before the term ends.

5. Invoice financing and factoring

Advances against unpaid invoices. Useful for B2B businesses with reliable clients who pay slowly. Fees add up quickly — calculate the annualized cost before committing.

6. Merchant cash advances

An advance repaid from a share of future card sales. Fast and easy to qualify for, but frequently among the most expensive forms of finance once converted to an annual rate. Treat it as a last resort.

7. Grants

Free money, with strings: grants are competitive, usually tied to a specific purpose (innovation, export, hiring, region, or under-represented founders) and often reimburse spending after the fact. Check national, regional and city economic-development programs, and beware of anyone charging a fee to "guarantee" a grant.

8. Investors

Angel investors and venture capital trade money for ownership. It suits businesses with large, fast growth potential. For most local small businesses, debt is cheaper than giving away equity.

9. Crowdfunding and community finance

Rewards-based crowdfunding pre-sells products to backers; community lenders and microloan programs serve businesses that banks overlook. Both double as marketing and validation.

How to improve your odds

  1. Separate business and personal finances and keep books current.
  2. Know your numbers: revenue trend, margins, cash flow and how the money will be repaid.
  3. Check your personal and business credit reports and fix errors.
  4. Prepare 3–6 months of bank statements and your latest tax returns.
  5. Apply before you're desperate.
