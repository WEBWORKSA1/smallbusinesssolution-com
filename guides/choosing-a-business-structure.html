Choosing a business structure

Your structure decides who is responsible for debts, how profits are taxed, and how much paperwork you'll do. Here is how the main options compare.

The four common structures

StructurePersonal liabilityTaxation (typical)Setup & admin
Sole proprietorshipUnlimited — you are the businessProfit taxed as your personal incomeLowest
PartnershipUsually shared and unlimited (general partners)Profit passes through to partnersLow, but needs a partnership agreement
LLC / limited companyGenerally limited to the business's assetsFlexible; often pass-through in the USModerate
CorporationLimited to the business's assetsCorporate tax, then tax on dividends; some regions offer small-business ratesHighest

Names and rules differ by country: an "LLC" is a US concept, while Canada, the UK, Australia and India use incorporated or private limited companies with their own tax treatment. Use this as a framework, then confirm locally.

Sole proprietorship: fast and simple

You can often start the same day with nothing more than a trade-name registration and any required licences. The trade-off is risk: business debts and lawsuits can reach your personal assets. It suits low-risk service businesses testing an idea.

Partnership: shared ownership, shared risk

Two or more people own the business together. Without a written agreement covering contributions, profit splits, decision-making and exit terms, partnerships are one of the most common sources of small business disputes. Write the agreement before you need it.

LLC or limited company: the popular middle ground

Limited liability means business debts generally stay with the business, provided you keep finances separate and don't personally guarantee obligations. Many lenders still ask owners for personal guarantees, so liability protection isn't absolute. Fees and annual filings are modest in most places.

Corporation: built for investors and scale

If you plan to raise equity investment, issue shares to employees, or retain large profits in the business, a corporation is often the right vehicle. Expect formal requirements: directors, minutes, separate tax returns and more accounting cost.

Five questions to decide

  1. Could a customer, employee or supplier realistically sue you for more than you could pay?
  2. Will you take on significant debt, leases or inventory?
  3. Do you expect profits high enough that a different tax treatment would save money?
  4. Will you bring in partners or investors?
  5. How much admin and accounting cost can you absorb this year?
If you answer "yes" to the first two questions, a limited-liability structure is usually worth the extra cost. If you answer "yes" to the third or fourth, talk to an accountant before choosing — the tax difference can be large.

Changing later

Many businesses start as sole proprietors and incorporate once revenue and risk grow. Converting is usually possible but can involve new registrations, bank accounts, contracts and tax filings, so it's worth planning ahead.
